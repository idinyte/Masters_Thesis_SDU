\subsection{Classical Approach to Control}

In 1961, Unimate, the first industrial robot, which worked on General Motors assembly line, was created. The Unimate was a hydraulically operated 5 degrees of freedom arm that performed pick-and-place tasks. The robot could be programmed by manually moving a robot arm to the a desired position and recording the position. Then the robot could replay the movement by moving to the same position. This resulted in the first of it's kind point-to-point movement which is still a standard feature in many robotic arms today. 
Such control method is quite simple, but it does require manual programming, meaning it is most suited for simple, repetitive tasks in well known environments. Besides that, the point-to-point movement has two obvious disadvantages. Firsty, the robot movement does not appear smooth, secondly, there must be no obstales between the points. These problems have been adressed in modern robotic control. The most simple way to move between two points is use linear interpolation. Similarly, orientation can be interpolated, depending on the representation chosen. For example, if orientation is represented as quaternions, a spherical linear interpolation (SLERP) can be used. This is because quaternion is a rotation representation on 4D unit sphere and in order to move between two points a sphere, the movement should be done on a following a geodesic. In theory linear interpolation results in constant velocity motion, but in practice this means that at the beginning of the motion robot will need an infinite acceleration to achieve desired velocity instantly and at the end of the movement, it will need infinite deacceleration to stop immediately. Objects with mass resist changes in motion due to inertia. The higher the object's mass is, the more force will be needed to accelerate it. The resistance to rotation also depends on the shape of the object, which is described by the moment of inertia. As robot is a system constrained by physical limitations, it will not experience infinite acceleration, but the motors will likely apply the maximum torque they are capable of during the brief moments at the beginning and the end of the motion. This increases wear and tear of the motor and other components, leading to the decreased lifespan of the robot. This issue can be mitigated by using a parabolic trajectory instead. Assuming the start velocity of zero and end velocity of zero, the parabolic trajectory will have a velocity profile that linearly increases until it reaches the half point of the trajectory and linearly decreases until it reaches zero again at the end of the trajectory. Consequently, the acceleration during first and second half of the movement will have much lower maximum value and will be constant, non-zero during majority of the motion. This leads to least wear and tear, but also slower movement. Fine control between trajectory duration and maximum acceleration can be achieved by merging these two methods. Linear trajectories with parabolic blends have a linear part in the middle and paranolic at the start end end of the interpolation.
