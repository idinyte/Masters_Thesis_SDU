\section{Background}

\subsection{Classical Approach to Robot Trajectory Generation}
In 1961, Unimate, the first industrial robot, which worked on the General Motors assembly line, was created. The Unimate was a hydraulically operated 5-degrees-of-freedom arm that performed pick-and-place tasks. The robot could be programmed by manually moving a robot arm to the desired position and recording the position. Then the robot could replay the movement by moving to the same position. This resulted in the first of its kind point-to-point movement, which is still a standard feature in many robotic arms today. 

\bigskip \noindent
Such a control method is quite simple; it is most suited for simple, repetitive tasks in well-known environments. The simplest way to move between two points is to use linear interpolation. Orientation can also be interpolated, but the interpolation method depends on the chosen orientation representation. For example, if orientation is represented as quaternions, a spherical linear interpolation (SLERP) \cite{shoemake1985animating} can be used. This is because a quaternion is a rotation representation on a 4D unit sphere, and in order to move between two points on a sphere in the shortest distance possible, the movement has to follow a geodesic. 

\bigskip \noindent
Linear interpolation results in a constant velocity motion. In practice, this means that at the beginning of the motion, the robot will need an infinite acceleration to achieve the desired velocity instantly, and at the end of the movement, it will need infinite deceleration to stop immediately. Objects with mass resist changes in motion due to inertia. The higher the object's mass is, the more force will be needed to accelerate it. The resistance to rotation also depends on the shape of the object, which is described by the moment of inertia. As a robot is a system constrained by physical limitations, infinite acceleration is not possible. Nonetheless, the motors will apply a high torque during the brief moments at the beginning and the end of the motion. This increases wear and tear on the motor and other components, leading to the decreased lifespan of the robot. This issue can be mitigated by using parabolic trajectories. Such a trajectory will have a linear increase and a decrease in its velocity profile instead of being constant throughout the whole trajectory. Consequently, the maximum acceleration will be smaller compared to just linear interpolation, leading to less wear and tear. A balance between trajectory duration and maximum acceleration can be achieved by merging these two methods. Linear trajectories with parabolic blends have a linear part in the middle and parabolic parts at the start and end of the interpolation.

\bigskip \noindent
There are many more interpolation methods. More natural-looking trajectories can be achieved by using splines. Splines ensure a smooth transition between path segments; they avoid abrupt changes in velocity and acceleration profiles. An easy way to implement splines in a path with many points is to use B-splines. This approach is recommended only for the parts in the trajectory that do not need to go through the point exactly, as B-splines are just an approximation of the path. If the robot has to move through the point in the path exactly, one of the interpolation methods should be used instead. 

\bigskip \noindent
The B-splines have a method for where to place an anchor point for fitting a Bezier curve. This methodical placement of anchor points is special in a way that it enables local control. Local control means that moving a single point in the trajectory will affect only that local portion of the trajectory, keeping the rest of the trajectory unaffected. The B-splines are easy to implement and have $C^2$ continuity. $C^2$ continuity (second-order continuity) ensures that the position, velocity, and acceleration in the trajectory are smooth. $C^0$ position continuity ensures that the end of one segment matches the start of another. $C^1$ velocity continuity ensures that the first derivatives at the end of one segment match the start of another. And $C^2$ acceleration continuity ensures that the second derivative matches at the junction points between the segments. It is possible to achieve even higher-order continuity; for example, quintic splines can achieve a $C^3$ jerk continuity or even higher if a higher-degree polynomial is used to interpolate between the points. 

\bigskip \noindent
Quintic splines, unlike B-splines, are an interpolation method. Quintic splines do come with several disadvantages, such as lack of local control and computational complexity, so the choice of splines used would depend on the use case scenario. Often interpolation methods are chosen for manually defined trajectories where precision is needed and approximation methods in recorded or noisy trajectories.


\bigskip \noindent
When moving between two points, it is not always clear if the robot will be able to move between them without collision. This is especially true with high-degree-of-freedom robotic arms, as even if the path for the end effector is clear, other robot parts may be in collision due to the configuration of the robot. It is a difficult problem to address efficiently due to the high dimensionality of the problem. Many modern robotics incorporate Rapidly-exploring Random Tree (RRT) \cite{lavalle1998rapidly} or RRT* \cite{karaman2011sampling} into their path planning algorithms to address this problem. RRT explores the robot's configuration space, which is a high-dimensional space that represents every possible configuration the robot can be in. C-Space can be divided into two sets: C-Free, a space with valid robot configurations, and C-Obs, a space where robot configuration would be in a collision. 

\begin{gather*}
C = C_{\text{free}} \cup C_{\text{obs}} \\
C_{\text{free}} \cap C_{\text{obs}} = \emptyset
\end{gather*}

\noindent
RRT can find a collision-free path between the two configurations in C-free space if one exists. The algorithm builds a tree-like structure by selecting a random point in a C-free space, connecting it to the closest node in a tree, and growing a branch from that tree towards the random point until it or the maximum step distance is reached. That point will be the new location for the node to be added into the tree if the shortest path between the node and the tree is obstacle-free. This process continues until a tree grows large enough so a path that connects the two points in configuration space is found. This method, however, does not yield the shortest path. RRT* addresses this issue by doing additional processing before placing a node in a tree. Within a specified search radius around the new point, RRT* checks if the nodes can be reconnected in a way that yields a shorter path. It has been proved that when the time goes to infinity, RRT* will yield the shortest path between two points by continuously rewiring the tree. 

\subsection{Neural Networks}
A neural network is a mathematical model inspired by biological neural network - the human brain (Fig. \ref{fig:artificial_and_human_neuron}). Neural networks are used in machine learning to achieve tasks that would be difficult or impossible by classical algorithms. The feedforward neural network \cite{lippmann1988introduction} was among the first types of neural networks. The information in such a network only flows forward from input to the output. Such a network is suitable for classification, regression, and pattern recognition tasks. Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) is the most common kind of feedforward neural network. It is divided into one-dimensional layers: an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer (Fig. \ref{fig:MLP}). If layers are fully connected, each neuron will be connected to every neuron in the previous layer. Modern networks can have millions and even billions of neurons; in such cases, weak connections are often pruned to optimize the network. The value of the neuron is calculated by multiplying inputs with weights and adding a bias Fig. \ref{fig:artificial_and_human_neuron}. As this operation is a linear function, the neurons need an additional activation function in order to learn non-linear patterns from the data.

\bigskip \noindent
A common and popular activation function is sigmoid $f(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$, which also clamps the neuron values between 0 and 1. In big networks, however, sigmoid adds a lot of additional computation; often ReLU \cite{nair2010rectified} is preferred. It is calculated simply as $f(x) = max(0, x)$. Clamping negative values is enough to introduce nonlinearity to the network. ReLU, however, sometimes presents another problem: if the network is poorly initialized with a lot of negative weights, or the learning rate is too high, or the data often leads to negative neuron activations, the neuron stops learning. This is called a dying ReLU problem, where too many neurons output zero for all inputs, effectively making them inactive. Leaky ReLU addresses this problem; instead of clamping negative values to zero, they are multiplied by a small constant, e.g. $\alpha=0.01$. 

\bigskip \noindent
For a multi-class classification problem, the logits (raw outputs of the last layer before applying the activation function) are passed through a softmax function $\text{Softmax}(z_i) = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j} e^{z_j}}$. The softmax function outputs the probabilities of input belonging to each class; the highest probability is often chosen as the network's prediction. The data for multi-class classification is usually one-hot encoded, where the probability of it belonging to the true class is 1 and 0 for the rest. The loss for such a problem is calculated using cross-entropy.

\[
\mathcal{L} = - \sum_{i} y_i \log(\hat{y}_i)
\]

\noindent
where:
\begin{itemize}
\item \( y_i \) is the true label (usually one-hot encoded, meaning one class is labeled as 1, others as 0).
\item \( \hat{y}_i \) is the predicted probability for class i from softmax.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The cross-entropy measures the difference between two probability distributions, leading to stronger gradients than using mean square error. For a one-hot encoded dataset, the cross-entropy simplifies to $\mathcal{L} = - \log(\hat{y}_{c})$ where $\hat{y}_c$ is the predicted probability of the true class. The goal of the neural network training process is to minimize this loss. This is done through a process called backpropagation. The weights and biases need to be adjusted in a way that leads to a lower loss. During the backpropagation, the gradients are calculated for each weight and bias by the use of the gradient chain rule. Then by taking a step for each weight and bias in the negative gradient direction, a loss will decrease. This process is repeated until either a local or global minimum is reached. When the network is big and there is a lot of data, it is impractical to use all of the data for each pass; often the data is split into smaller batches before being passed through the network. Another way a neural network training process is sped up is instead of using the vanilla gradient descent, a modern optimizer like Adam is often employed. Adam (Adaptive Momentum Estimation) improves the gradient descent with two key ideas. Firstly, it incorporates momentum by using the mean of past gradients. This helps the optimizer move in a constant direction and to reduce oscillations. Secondly, it optimizes the step size for each parameter individually based on past updates. This results in faster convergence, less sensitivity to noisy gradients (which can occur in mini-batch training), and less sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning.

This makes RNNs ideal for tasks where order matters, such as language modeling or speech recognition. For image recognition and feature extraction tasks Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) \cite{krizhevsky2012imagenet} are often employed. It is the part of the network that captures the esential features of the input data.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning}
Reinforcement learning (RL) is a subset of machine learning. There are three main types of machine learning: supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning. Supervised learning has labeled data; a model finds patterns that relate to labeled input-output data, and once trained, it can use those patterns to predict outcomes not yet known. Unsupervised learning has no labeled data or human feedback; instead, it finds hidden structure in the data itself and can be used to cluster it. RL learns through trial and error by interacting with the environment, receiving feedback in the form of reward or punishment \ref{fig:reinforcement_learning_model}. It is ideal for tasks that require sequential decision-making, such as playing games, navigating self-driving cars, or controlling robots, just to name a few.

\subsubsection{Terminology}

\noindent
In order to understand RL, it is important to understand the key concepts and the terminology:

\bigskip \noindent
Agent: the agent is the decision maker. In the context of a game, it would be a player, and in the context of this thesis, it will be a robot arm.

\bigskip \noindent
Environment: it is a place in which the agent resides. It encompasses everything that the agent perceives and acts upon.

\bigskip \noindent
Action: actions are choices available to the agent in a given state. The actions can be discrete, where the number of moves the agent can perform is finite; for example, two actions could be to open or close the robot gripper. The actions can also be continuous; they are often represented by a real number or a vector of real numbers. Robot's actions are often continuous, such as opening a gripper to a certain distance or moving the robot's joint to a certain angle. Reinforcement learning methods and approaches often differ depending on if the actions are discrete or continuous. 

\bigskip \noindent
Action space: a set of all valid actions in a given environment.

\bigskip \noindent
Reward: the feedback from the environment. A numerical value associated with how good the agent's action was. A positive value represents a reward, and a negative value represents a punishment. It depends on the state, action, and the next state of the environment $r_t = R(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})$. Although frequently the reward dependence on inputs is simplified only to state-action pair $r_t = R(s_t, a_t)$ or even the current state only $r_t = R(s_t)$. The agent's goal is to maximize the cumulative reward. Designing a reward function that aligns with the desired behavior of the agent is a challenging task often requiring lots of trial and error. Another common difficulty is rewards often being sparse; for example, the winner in the game of chess is only decided after the game has ended; it is difficult for the agent to know which of the moves led the agent to the victory or loss. Sparse rewards make the agent learn at a much slower rate, leading to extensive training times.

\bigskip \noindent
Discount factor: $\gamma \in [0,1] $ is a parameter that discounts the future rewards. Total cumulative reward over trajectory $\tau$ with discount factor is calculated as $R(\tau) = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t r_t$. A discount factor is introduced for a couple of reasons. Firstly, an infinite-horizon sum of rewards may not converge to a finite value, and secondly, it is often desirable to tune if the agent should prioritize strategies that benefit it in the long term or short term. If the discount factor is high (e.g. 0.9 - 0.99) the agent will try to learn long-term strategies, and if it is low, it will focus on quick gains.

\bigskip \noindent
Policy: the policy is what defines the agent's strategy. It is a mapping between states and actions. There are two types of policies, deterministic and stochastic. An agent following a deterministic policy will always choose the same action given the same state. On the other hand, an agent following a stochastic policy will have a probability distribution over the available actions.

\bigskip \noindent
Value function: it represents an expected cumulative reward for a certain state or state-action pair if the agent were to act according to the same policy forever after. It shows how good a particular state or action is. State-value function $V(s)$: represents expected reward starting in a state s. Action-value function $Q(s, a)$: represents expected reward for taking action a in state s and following the policy thereafter. The action-value function is also known as the Q-function.

\bigskip \noindent
Exploration vs. Exploitation: it is a key challenge in RL on how to balance between exploration and exploitation. Exploitation means the agent chooses the best-known action in order to maximize its reward. Exploration means the agent tries less-known actions in order to discover new, potentially better strategies. There are several exploration vs. exploitation balancing strategies; one that is simple and well known is called $\epsilon - Greedy$. During the beginning of the training, $\epsilon \in [0,1]$ is initialized to a high value (e.g., 1), making the agent perform mostly random actions, and as training continues, epsilon decreases, making the robot prefer to exploit the policy (Eq. \ref{eq:e-greedy}).

\begin{equation}
a =
\begin{cases} 
\text{random action}, & \text{with probability } \varepsilon \\
\arg\max_{a} Q(s, a), & \text{with probability } 1 - \varepsilon
\end{cases}
\label{eq:e-greedy}
\end{equation}

\bigskip \noindent
On-policy and off-policy learning. These are the two ways the policies can be categorized. On-policy learning refers to a process when the agent continuously updates the same policy it uses to interact with the environment. On-policy learning often results in more stable but much slower training compared to off-policy training. Off-policy training refers to a process when an agent learns from experiences collected using a different policy than the one it is currently improving. The agent saves the necessary representation for learning to a replay buffer after each action it takes. During the training loop, a number of random experiences are sampled from the replay buffer and used to update the agent's policy. This parallelism of using many experiences at each iteration loop allows the agent to learn quickly, but the learning may be less stable.

\bigskip \noindent
Model-Based and Model-Free Learning. Approaches to RL differ depending on if a model of the environment (transition probabilities $ P(s_{t+1} | s, a)$ and rewards $r_t$) is given. Tasks where the model is known are less common and usually applicable to smaller problems. The model-free learning is a more general case.

\subsubsection{Markov Decision Process}
Markov Decision Process (MDP) is the backbone of reinforcement learning. Problems that are modeled as MDPs assume the Markov property, which states that the probability of transitioning to the next state \( s_{t+1} \) depends only on the current state-action pair \( (s_t, a_t) \), and not on the full history of previous states and actions:

\begin{align}
P(s_{t+1} | s_t, a_t, s_{t-1}, a_{t-1}, \dots, s_0, a_0) = P(s_{t+1} | s_t, a_t)
\end{align}

\noindent
If the environment is only partially observable or depends on past states, the problem cannot be modeled as an MDP. The MDP framework models the environment as a tuple \( (S, A, P, R, \gamma) \):

\begin{itemize}
\item \( S \) Set of all states
\item \( A \) Set of all actions
\item \( P(s' | s, a) \) Probability of transitioning to state \( s' \) given state \( s \) and action \( a \)
\item \( R(s, a, s') \) Reward function
\item \( \gamma \in [0,1] \) Discount factor
\end{itemize}

\noindent
This framework allows mathematical expression of reinforcement learning problems. The expected return of a policy \( \pi \) can be described using value functions, which satisfy the recursive Bellman equations. The Bellman equations for on-policy methods are:

\begin{align}
V^\pi(s) &= \mathbb{E}_\pi \left[ r_{t+1} + \gamma V^\pi(S_{t+1}) \mid S_t = s \right] \\
Q^\pi(s, a) &= \mathbb{E}_\pi \left[ r_{t+1} + \gamma Q^\pi(S_{t+1}, A_{t+1}) \mid S_t = s, A_t = a \right]
\end{align}

\noindent
These describe the expected return when following policy \( \pi \). Actions are sampled from the policy \( a \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s) \), and future states from the environment dynamics \( s_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s, a) \). The Bellman equations for optimal policy are:

\begin{align}
V^*(s) &= \max_a \mathbb{E} \left[ r_{t+1} + \gamma V^*(S_{t+1}) \mid S_t = s, A_t = a \right] \\
Q^*(s, a) &= \mathbb{E} \left[ r_{t+1} + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^*(S_{t+1}, a') \mid S_t = s, A_t = a \right]
\end{align}

\noindent
These define the maximum achievable return when the agent always chooses the best possible action. Bellman equations are foundational because they enable dynamic programming methods such as value iteration and policy iteration for computing optimal policies. Solving Bellman equations exactly is often not possible in large or continuous spaces, in that case approximate methods are used.

\subsubsection{Overview of the Reinforcement Learning Methods}
Problems that could be characterized by small MDPs are often solved using dynamic programming or temporal-difference (TD) updates. The states and actions from these low-dimensional problems can often be stored in a simple table. A classic algorithms Q-learning \cite{watkins_q-learning_1992} is an example of such method. Over time, function approximation with the use of neural networks extended RL to larger state spaces. The deep RL era was launched by the Deep Q-Network (DQN) \cite{mnih_human-level_2015}. It achieved human-level Atari game performance by using a convolutional neural net to approximate Q-values from raw pixel data. This success of function approximation spurred many deep RL algorithms that use neural nets for policies or value functions.

\bigskip \noindent
Methods of reinforcement learning can be put in three main categories: Value-based, Actor-Critic, and Policy Gradient (Fig. \ref{fig:reinforcement_learning_categories}).

\begin{itemize}
  \item Value-based methods learn a value V(s) or action-value Q(a,s) function. From these functions a policy is derived by selecting actions with highest values (e.g. greedy or $\epsilon-greedy$). Value based methods are simple and stable for discrete action spaces, but require discretization or function approximation for continuous action spaces. They also do not perfrom well for stochastic policies. 
  \item Policy gradient methods learn policy directly, without relying on value functions. The policy is typically parametrized. By computing gradients of the expected reward with respect to the policy parameters the policy is trained using gradient ascent. These methods can handle continuous action spaces and stochastic policies. Stohastic policies are particularly useful for partially observable environments and increased exploration. On the other hand, policy-based methods tend to have high variance in gradient estimates, which can lead to unstable training. Also these methods often converge to local optima, leading to suboptimal policies, so careful parameter tuning is required.
  \item Actor-Critic methods combine ideas from both categories. They have a policy (actor) which is updated using feedback from a learned value function (critic). This critic's guidace reduces variance in actor's updates compared to purely policy gradient based methods. Actor-Critic methods can be flexible for both discrete and continuous action spaces, but due two different interacting components these methods can be more complex to implement and can be sensitive to the choice of hyperparemeters.
\end{itemize}

Modern robot control that uses RL for tasks such as pick-and-place uses continuous-action deep RL. A major challenge for a lot of these tasks is sparse rewards. The issue is that the agent does not know what to improve from failed attempts, it mostly learns from successes. Hindsight Experience Replay (HER) \cite{andrychowicz2018hindsightexperiencereplay} addresses this issue. The key idea for this method is to change the goal for failed attempts and pretend that that the goal it actually achieved was the goal all along. This significantly improving sample efficiency in many robotic tasks like pushing, sliding, or pick-and-place. The method works only for off-policy algorithms due to the need of replay buffer to manipulate past experiences. The robot training is usually done in simulation and transferred to real robots afterwards. Survey paper \cite{robotics10030105} note that while many algorithms have been tested, no single method stands out as an industry standard for pick-and-place tasks. The performance of algorithms vary based on task complexity, scene clutter, and the training environment, online vs simulation. SAC, PPO and DDPG are popular choices that are often combined with HER method for tasks that rely on sparse rewards.

\subsection{Learning from Demonstration}
RL generalizes to many applications requiring decision-making, but often it is difficult to design a reward function. Learning from Demonstration (LfD), also known as imitation learning, addresses this challenge by using expert demonstrations instead of a manually defined reward function. In LfD, an expert provides examples of how to perform a task, and the agent learns a policy that aims to imitate the expert's behavior. The environment in imitation learning is often defined as a Markov Decision Process. The environment has a set of states and actions, transition probabilities between the states, and an unknown reward function. The expert demonstrations, also known as trajectories, are defined as a sequence of state-action pairs. 

\begin{align}
\tau &= (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \dots)
\end{align}

\noindent
The expert demonstrations are assumend to be coming from an optimal policy $\pi^*$. There are a several approaches to LfD. In this section, two common methods will be discussed: behavior cloning (BC) and inverse reinforcement learning (IRL).

\subsubsection{Behaviour Cloning}
The idea of behavior cloning \cite{bain1995framework} is relatively straightforward. Given expert trajectories, learn a mapping from states to actions using supervised learning. A commonly used example to explain behavior cloning consists of a radio-controlled (RC) car equipped with sensors that is being controlled on a race track. A human expert would drive around the racetrack recording the state (camera feedback, speed) and actions (steering, acceleration). The training would consist of a neural network whose inputs are states from the expert trajectory and outputs are predicted actions. The network then would try to minimize the loss between predicted and expert actions. Behavioral cloning is a simple method, suited for tasks that do not require long-term planning and in which expert trajectories cover the whole state space. On the other hand, BC has severe limitations; it can only get as good as the expert, the agent can only perform well in situations it has seen before, and the generalization is poor. Often the agent does not match the expert trajectory perfectly, leading to an accumulation of error over time. Once the error grows large, the agent can find itself in a situation it has never seen before; this will lead to failure as the agent will not know what to do in novel states. 

\bigskip \noindent
The paper "ChauffeurNet: Learning to Drive by Imitating the Best and Synthesizing the Worst" \cite{bansal2018chauffeurnet} proposes that the network is trained not only on the expert trajectories, but the dataset is augmented with simulated uncommon scenarios, such as collisions and off-road events in the case of training a car to drive autonomously. Another interesting proposition \cite{goecks2020integratingbehaviorcloningreinforcement} is the Cycle-of-Learning (CoL) framework, where behavior cloning and reinforcement learning are combined by pre-training the agent from expert demonstrations and fine-tuning it through RL.

\subsubsection{Inverse Reinforcement Learning}
The inverse reinforcement learning takes a different approach to imitation learning. The main idea of this method is to learn the reward function instead of the policy directly. This learned reward function then can be used to train the agent through the reinforcement learning. 

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} Expert demonstrations $D = \{\tau_1, \tau_2,\dots, \tau_n\}$
\Repeat
\State Learn reward function $r(s, a)$
\State Given reward function $r$ find policy $\pi$ using the reinforcement learning approach
\State Compare $\pi$ with expert policy $\pi_E$
\Until{Policy is satisfactory}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent
In model-based approaches, the reward function is often assumed to be a linear combination of known features. At each iteration of IRL, a full RL problem is solved using approaches such as dynamic programming (DP) to compute the optimal policy under the current reward estimate. In the model-free approach, the reward function is often represented as a non-linear function (e.g., using neural networks), and transition probabilities are unknown; in this case, the reward function is continuously updated using short-horizon updates (e.g., 1-step policy evaluation rollouts) to estimate gradients and update the reward function incrementally.

\bigskip \noindent
The inverse reinforcement learning has very good generalization, meaning, unlike the behavior cloning, IRL can better deal with scenarios that were not explicitly shown by the expert as the model learns the goal rather than state-action mappings. Sometimes the policy trained with IRL can even outperform the expert. It is a good choice for more complex scenarios requiring long-term planning or decision-making. On the other hand, the IRL has problems of its own. The biggest is ambiguity in the reward function. There can be many reward functions that explain the behavior of the expert. Different approaches have been proposed on which reward function to choose.

\bigskip \noindent
Maximum Margin Inverse Reinforcement Learning (MM-IRL) \cite{ratliff2006maximum} envisions the problem as structured prediction over the space of policies. The goal of MM-IRL is to learn a cost function, which is assumed to be a linear combination of features. The features are problem specific; for example, in a robot pick-and-place task, they could be the time taken to achieve a task, applied grasping force, smoothness of motion, etc. The cost function is just a negative reward function. The cost function is assumed to be linear in the features.

\begin{align}
R(s,a) &= -C(s,a) \\
C(s,a) &= w^T \phi(s,a)
\end{align}

\noindent
where:
\begin{itemize}
\item \( C(s,a) \) is the cost function,
\item \( w \) is the weight vector that must be learned,
\item \( \phi(s,a) \) are the features describing state-action pairs,
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Knowing the cost function is akin to knowing the reward function; using it, the policy can be learned. MM-IRL is a model-based method; solving the optimization requires access to environment dynamics. The problem can be viewed as a constrained optimization problem, where the goal is to find the set of weights $w$ that explain the expert trajectory. More specifically, the cost of the expert trajectory has to be smaller or equal to the cost of all the possible trajectories in MDP. This optimization problem can not be solved by a linear solver, as the trivial solution $w = 0$ would satisfy the constraint. To avoid this, MM-IRL introduces a margin: the cost of expert trajectories has to be smaller than the cost of all of the remaining trajectories by a fixed margin. In order to account for cases where the set of weights is very large (and thus the margin term becomes small), an additional constraint is added that maximizes the margin.
